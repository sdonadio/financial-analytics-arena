WEEK 2

Factor Models & Cross-Sectional Regression

A return is not a number, it is a sum: so much of it is the market, so much is small-versus-large, so much is cheap-versus-expensive, and whatever is left over is the part somebody wants to be paid for. This week you take that sum apart — and then you learn how easy it is to manufacture the leftover.

  • Fit and defend a CAPM regression: beta, alpha, the standard error of each, and the residual plot you are supposed to look at.
  • Compute rolling betas and say out loud what your window length is trading off.
  • Run a Fama-French 3-factor attribution of a fund’s returns, and report how much of its “alpha” was a value tilt with a good story.
  • Say how long a track record has to be before an alpha of a given size is even detectable — and what happens to that threshold when you have tested a hundred strategies.
Lab: FF factors from the Ken French library, rolling betas on 20 names HW2: factor attribution of a fund — “is this alpha?” Read the diagnostics, not the R²
Part 1

1What is it? One regression, and what each coefficient is claiming

A factor model is a regression of excess returns on the excess returns of things you could have bought instead. The slopes say what you were exposed to; the intercept says what you got that the exposures do not explain. Both are estimates, both have standard errors, and the intercept’s standard error is the single number that decides whether the week’s headline claim survives.

CAPM, as a regression you can run

ri,t − rft = α + β (rm,t − rft) + εt

β is the slope: how much of the market you were carrying. α is the intercept: average excess return left after paying for that exposure at the market’s own price. ε is what is left each day, and sd(ε) annualised is tracking error — the denominator of every information ratio you will ever quote.

The identity that matters. t(α) = (α/σε) × √T — the information ratio times the square root of the sample length. Nothing else. Everything in Part 4 follows from those three symbols.

Fama-French, as two more columns

ri − rf = α + βm·MKT + βs·SMB
             + βv·HML + ε

The three-factor model adds SMB (small minus big) and HML (high book-to-market minus low). The five-factor model adds profitability (RMW) and investment (CMA); momentum (UMD) is usually bolted on separately.

The point is not the R². The point is that a fund’s CAPM alpha of 300 bps routinely becomes an FF3 alpha of 40 bps, because the manager was holding cheap small companies and those have a published price. What survives that is the claim.

2How you use it — rolling exposures and a real attribution

A single full-sample beta is a claim that the fund never changed. It always did. So the working tool is a rolling regression, and the working deliverable is an attribution table that says how much of the return each exposure bought at the market’s own price.

3Why it matters: how long before an alpha is even detectable?

  
This is the section that changes how you read a pitch. An alpha is not a number you
  measure, it is a hypothesis you test, and the test has a power. With a 0.25 information ratio you need
  sixty-four years of data for a two-standard-error result — which is longer than the
  fund has existed, longer than the manager will work, and longer than the strategy will survive.

What this changes about the decision

      
An allocator does not fund alphas, they fund claims about alphas.
      A 300 bps CAPM alpha that becomes 40 bps under FF3 is not a manager to hire, it is a
      value ETF with a fee attached — and the correct decision is to buy the ETF. That is a real,
      repeated, multi-billion-dollar conclusion and it comes out of one regression with three extra
      columns.

      
The same logic runs backwards: a fund with a negative market
      loading and a small positive alpha can be worth far more than its headline return, because you can
      lever it. Loadings are the whole conversation.

    

    

      
What this changes about your own project

      
Phase 2 asks for an explicit overfitting section stating how many configurations you
      tried. That is not a confession, it is a parameter of the test: without N, a
      t-stat has no threshold, and a Sharpe has no meaning. Keep the log as you go — reconstructing it
      afterwards is both harder and less honest.

      
Week 7 turns this into deflated Sharpe, and week 8 into purged cross-validation. Both
      are the same idea as this page, applied to a search rather than a single test.

    

  







  
Part 4

  
4Five ways this goes wrong

  
Every one of these has been submitted with a straight face. Open them.


  
Reporting R² as if it were the result
    

      
“My FF3 regression has an R² of 0.94, so the model is good.” An R² of 0.94 on a long-only equity
      fund is not a finding, it is arithmetic: the fund holds equities and so does MKT. You can get 0.9
      by regressing a fund on itself with noise added.

      
Worse, a high R² and a large alpha usually means the alpha is being carried
      by a handful of outlier months, which the R² cannot see.

      
Fix: report the coefficients with standard errors, plot the residuals against time
      and against fitted values, and look for the three months doing all the work. R² belongs in a
      footnote.

    


  
Regressing raw returns instead of excess returns
    

      
CAPM is a statement about excess returns. Subtract the risk-free rate from
      both sides or the intercept absorbs rf·(1 − β), which in a 5% rates regime is
      hundreds of basis points of entirely fake alpha for any fund with a beta below 1.

      
The Ken French factor files already give you Mkt-RF and RF as separate
      columns, in percent per day, which is the other half of this bug: mixing percent and decimal is the
      single most common cause of a beta of 0.0001.

      
Fix: build one function that returns excess returns in decimals and use it
      everywhere. Assert that abs(factors).max() < 0.3 before regressing — if it fails,
      you are in percent.

    


  
Overlapping windows, and t-stats that believe them
    

      
Rolling betas from a 126-day window on daily data are massively autocorrelated: consecutive
      estimates share 125 of 126 observations. Any test that treats a series of rolling estimates as
      independent observations — “beta was above 1.2 in 400 of 800 days, so the shift is significant” —
      is inflating its sample size by two orders of magnitude.

      
The same trap applies to overlapping k-day forward returns in a predictive regression, which is
      week 8’s version of the problem.

      
Fix: Newey-West standard errors (cov_type="HAC" in statsmodels), or
      non-overlapping windows, or both. And never count rolling estimates as data points.

    


  
A factor zoo, fitted to a five-year sample
    

      
Twelve factors, sixty monthly observations. The regression will run, the R² will be lovely, and
      every loading will be a linear combination of the others: MKT, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA, UMD, BAB, QMJ and
      a low-vol factor are not nine independent things.

      
The tells are enormous standard errors, loadings that flip sign when you add one more month, and
      a condition number in the thousands.

      
Fix: start at three factors, add one only when it survives on its own and improves
      the residual, and always print the variance inflation factors. Ten
      observations per regressor is a floor, not a target.

    


  
“I only tested one strategy” — after testing forty
    

      
The most human of these. You tried a 20-day lookback, then 10, then 50; you tried it on the
      S&P 500 and then on the Russell; you dropped 2008 because it was “not representative”. That is
      not one test, and the 1.96 threshold no longer applies to it — the relevant threshold for 40 tests
      is about 3.2.

      
Every one of those choices was made after seeing data, which is what makes
      them tests rather than design decisions.

      
Fix: a log file, appended to as you go, with one line per configuration you ran.
      Then a Bonferroni or Benjamini-Hochberg threshold computed from its length, and a deflated Sharpe in
      week 7. Publish N. It is the cheapest credibility you will ever buy.
