WEEK 4

Risk Analytics

A risk number is not a measurement. It is a forecast — a claim about a distribution you have never seen, made from a sample of the one you have. This week you produce three of those forecasts for the same portfolio, watch them disagree by 40%, and then do the only thing that settles the argument: count how often each one was wrong.

  • Compute VaR and Expected Shortfall three ways — historical, Gaussian, EWMA — on the portfolio you optimised in week 3, and say which assumption each one is really making.
  • Fit a GARCH(1,1) with arch, read α+β as a persistence half-life, and forecast a vol term structure instead of a single number.
  • Backtest your own risk model with a Kupiec unconditional-coverage test and a Basel traffic light — and accept the verdict when it is red.
  • Stress the book on March 2020, report max drawdown and time-to-recover, and distinguish a drawdown from a loss.

Part 1

1What is it? Four numbers, and only one of them is a fact

Risk analytics is the business of summarising a return distribution into something a committee can act on. Realised volatility is the only one of this week's numbers that is simply a property of the sample you have. VaR, Expected Shortfall and a GARCH forecast are all statements about tomorrow, and each is only as good as the shape it assumes the tail has.

Volatility, realised

The standard deviation of the sample, annualised by ×√252. Backward-looking, model-free, and roughly symmetric — which is exactly why it is a poor description of an equity book, whose left tail is much longer than its right.

VaRα

The loss you exceed with probability 1−α. A quantile, so it says nothing about how bad the breach is when it happens. Not sub-additive in general: the VaR of a portfolio can exceed the sum of its parts, which makes it a bad object to allocate limits with.

Expected Shortfall

The mean loss given that VaR was breached. Coherent, sub-additive, and the Basel standard since FRTB. Harder to backtest, because you are estimating a conditional mean from the handful of observations you have in the tail.

The pedagogical point of the whole week: two portfolios can have identical volatility and completely different risk. The widget below builds exactly that pair — same 17.5% annualised vol, one Gaussian, one with a fat left tail — and shows you that the 99% VaR moves by more than a third while the vol number never budges.

Part 2

2How you build it — three estimators over one series

All three VaR estimators consume the same object: the daily P&L series of the week-3 portfolio, (w * ret).sum(axis=1). They differ in one line each, and that line is the whole model. Write all three, always, and report the spread between them as a measure of model risk.

The three lines

pnl = (ret * w).sum(axis=1)      # week-3 portfolio
a   = 0.99

# 1 · historical — no shape assumption
var_h = -pnl.quantile(1 - a)

# 2 · Gaussian — shape assumed, tail understated
z     = norm.ppf(1 - a)
var_g = -(pnl.mean() + z * pnl.std())

# 3 · EWMA (RiskMetrics, lam=0.94)
v     = pnl.pow(2).ewm(alpha=1-0.94).mean()
var_e = -z * np.sqrt(v.iloc[-1])

# ES: the average of the breaches
es_h  = -pnl[pnl <= -var_h].mean()

Three lines, three different answers, and the ordering between them is informative: var_e above the other two means today is a high-vol day and your unconditional estimates are stale.

What each one is really assuming

  • Historical. That the past n days are an i.i.d. draw from tomorrow's distribution. No shape assumption — and no ability to see a loss larger than the worst one in the window. Your 99% VaR from 250 days is the 2.5th-worst day; it is an order statistic, with the variance of one.
  • Gaussian. That the tail is thin. Fast, differentiable, aggregates cleanly across a book — and understates the 99% loss on equities by 20–40% in a typical sample.
  • EWMA. That vol is persistent and the shape is Gaussian. It reacts to a regime change in days rather than months, which is why it is the estimator that gets a risk limit cut in time.
  • GARCH. EWMA plus mean reversion: the extra parameter ω pulls the forecast back to a long-run level, so the term structure has a shape instead of being flat.

Part 3

3Why it matters: a risk model is only as good as its exceedance count

Anyone can publish a VaR. The question a reviewer, a regulator and this course all ask is the same: how often was it wrong, and was that the right number of times? A 99% one-day VaR should be breached about 2.5 times a year. Two breaches is fine. Zero is not — a model that never breaches is a model that is over-reserving capital and mispricing every limit downstream.

The Kupiec unconditional-coverage test

With T tests, x breaches and coverage p = 1−α, the likelihood-ratio statistic is

LR = -2*log((1-p)**(T-x) * p**x)
     +2*log((1-x/T)**(T-x) * (x/T)**x)

which is χ²(1) under the null, so LR > 3.84 rejects at 5%. It tests the number of breaches only. Christoffersen's independence test asks the second question — whether they cluster — and a model can pass Kupiec while failing that badly.

The Basel traffic light

Over 250 business days at 99%, the supervisory response is mechanical: green for 0–4 breaches, yellow for 5–9 with an escalating capital multiplier, red at 10 or more, which means the model is presumed broken. That is the entire industry's answer to "is your risk model any good", and it is a counting exercise.

The lesson for a tearsheet: report the count, the expectation and the test — never a lone VaR number. A VaR without a backtest is a decoration.

Part 4

4Five ways this goes wrong

Every one of these has appeared in a submitted risk report. Open them.

Scaling a one-day VaR by √10 and calling it a ten-day VaR

The square-root-of-time rule requires i.i.d. returns. Returns are not i.i.d.: volatility is persistent, so the ten-day variance is larger than ten times the one-day variance whenever vol is autocorrelated, and the ten-day quantile is further out still because the aggregated distribution is skewed.

In a GARCH world with α+β = 0.98, scaling from a calm day understates the ten-day 99% VaR by 15–25%. The error is largest exactly when it matters, because that is when the starting point is unrepresentative.

Fix: simulate the horizon. Draw ten-day paths from your fitted GARCH (or block bootstrap ten-day windows from history), then take the quantile of the aggregated P&L. If you must scale, say so and report the bias you measured on your own data.

Backtesting the model in sample, on the window it was fitted to

Estimate σ over the full sample, compute a 99% VaR from it, then count breaches over the same full sample. You will get close to 1% almost by construction — the quantile was chosen to make that true. It is a tautology dressed as validation.

The same error in a subtler form: choosing the EWMA λ that minimises the breach count, then reporting that breach count as evidence the model works.

Fix: rolling, strictly out of sample. Estimate on days [t-250, t), test on day t, advance. If you tuned a parameter, tune it on the first half and test on the second, and say how many values you tried.

Reporting Expected Shortfall from four observations

Historical 97.5% ES over 250 days is the mean of the worst six days. Historical 99% ES over 250 days is the mean of the worst two or three. The standard error on that mean is enormous — add one new bad day and your ES moves by 20%.

Students then compare last quarter's ES to this quarter's, find a 15% change, and write a paragraph explaining it. There is nothing to explain: that is sampling noise.

Fix: either estimate ES parametrically from a fitted tail (Student-t or a generalised-Pareto fit above a threshold), or bootstrap a confidence interval and publish it. An ES of "$412k (95% CI $310–560k)" is an honest number; "$412k" is not.

A GARCH fit with α+β > 1, shipped anyway

arch will happily return a fit whose persistence exceeds one, usually because the sample contains one structural break (a merger, a redenomination, 2020-03) that the model absorbs by making variance non-stationary. The long-run variance ω/(1−α−β) is then negative or infinite, and every multi-day forecast diverges to nonsense.

Its cousin: α+β = 0.999, which is technically stationary and has a shock half-life of 693 days. That is IGARCH — a random walk in variance — and it means your "long-run vol" is doing no work at all.

Fix: check res.params before you use the fit. Assert alpha+beta < 0.999, look at the half-life ln(0.5)/ln(α+β) and ask whether it is plausible, and refit on a sample split around any suspected break. Report the persistence next to every vol forecast.

Confusing volatility with risk — and drawdown with loss

A short-volatility book has low realised vol until the day it does not: measured σ is 6% a year for three years and the fourth year takes 40%. Any risk system driven by trailing volatility sizes that position up right before the event, because low vol is being read as low risk.

The mirror error is reporting max drawdown as though it were a loss. A 30% drawdown that recovered cost a buy-and-hold investor nothing and cost a leveraged or redeeming one everything. Which it was depends on the path and the liability, not on the number.

Fix: report vol, skew, kurtosis, VaR, ES and drawdown together, and add one stress scenario that is not in your sample. For any strategy with option-like payoffs, size on the stress result, not on the trailing volatility.

6Do the work

In the lab (~75 min)

  • Take the week-3 portfolio weights, build the daily P&L series, and compute VaR and ES three ways at 95% and 99%. Tabulate all twelve numbers.
  • Fit a GARCH(1,1) with arch. Report α, β, persistence and half-life, and plot the 60-day vol forecast against the unconditional level.
