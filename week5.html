WEEK 5

Rates, Inflation & the Macro Panel

Every price in this course is a discounted cash flow, and the discount comes from one object: the curve. This week you build it from free FRED data, take it apart into the three components that explain 98% of every move it has ever made, and read the two things it is quietly telling you — what the market expects inflation to be, and what it thinks the odds of default are.

  • Bootstrap discount factors and zero rates from quoted par yields, and know why the zero curve is above the par curve when the curve slopes up.
  • Compute duration and convexity and measure exactly where the linear approximation stops being good enough to hedge with.
  • Turn a nominal and a real yield into a TIPS breakeven, then argue about how much of it is expected inflation and how much is premium.
  • PCA the curve into level, slope and curvature, reconstruct a real curve move from three numbers, and see what that buys a hedger.
  • Read a credit spread as an implied default probability under a recovery assumption — and see how much of it is not compensation for default at all.

Part 1

1What is it? Four representations of exactly one thing

"The yield curve" is a single object with four faces, and every mistake in this week's homework comes from using one where another was meant. The par curve is what gets quoted. The discount-factor curve is what you actually multiply cash flows by. The zero curve is the same information expressed as a rate. The forward curve is what those rates imply about the future — and it is not a forecast.

Par yield y(t)

The coupon that makes a bond of that maturity price at 100. It is a single number standing in for a whole schedule of cash flows, so it is a weighted average of the zero rates along the way — which is why it lies below the zero curve on an upward-sloping curve.

Discount factor DF(t)

The present value of $1 at t. Model-free, always positive, always decreasing in a positive-rate world. This is the object you should store and interpolate: no compounding convention, no day count, nothing to get wrong.

Zero rate z(t)

DF(t) = (1+z(t))−t. The yield on a bond with one cash flow. Every valuation in the course discounts with these, not with par yields — discounting a ten-year bond's five-year coupon at the ten-year par yield is simply wrong.

Forward rate f(t₁,t₂)

The rate you can lock in today for a future period: DF(t₁)/DF(t₂) − 1. It is an arbitrage relationship, not an expectation. A steep forward curve means carry, not a prediction that rates will rise.

Part 2

2How you build it — bootstrap, differentiate, then subtract

Three computations carry this week. The bootstrap turns quotes into discount factors. Duration and convexity are the first and second derivatives of price with respect to yield, and they are what a risk system actually stores instead of a full revaluation. The breakeven is a subtraction between two curves that you can pull from FRED in two lines.

FRED to a curve, and the bootstrap

import pandas_datareader.data as web

# nominal Treasury constant-maturity yields
ids = ["DGS1", "DGS2", "DGS3", "DGS5",
       "DGS7", "DGS10", "DGS20", "DGS30"]
y = web.DataReader(ids, "fred",
                   "2003-01-01") / 100

# bootstrap annual-coupon par yields -> discount factors
df, acc = [], 0.0
for n, yn in enumerate(par, start=1):
    d = (1 - yn * acc) / (1 + yn)
    df.append(d); acc += d
z = [d ** (-1 / (i + 1)) - 1
     for i, d in enumerate(df)]

Two FRED series ids are the whole macro panel: DGS* for nominal, DFII* for real (TIPS). No key, no scraping, and T10YIE even publishes the breakeven so you can check your own subtraction.

Duration, convexity, and what they are for

t  = np.arange(1, T + 1)
cf = np.full(T, c * 100); cf[-1] += 100
d  = (1 + y) ** -t
P  = cf @ d

D  = (t * cf * d).sum() / P        # Macaulay
MD = D / (1 + y)                  # modified
C  = (t * (t + 1) * cf * d).sum() / (
     P * (1 + y) ** 2)            # convexity

dP = -MD * P * dy + 0.5 * C * P * dy**2

Modified duration is the percentage price move per 1% yield move; DV01 = MD × P / 10000 is the same thing in dollars per basis point, and it is the unit every rates desk speaks in. Convexity is the correction that makes a 200 bp move hedgeable.

The breakeven is the inflation rate at which a nominal Treasury and a TIPS of the same maturity return the same thing. It is a subtraction, which makes it easy to compute and easy to over-interpret: it is expected inflation plus an inflation-risk premium minus a TIPS liquidity premium, and in March 2020 the last term did most of the moving.

Part 3

3Why it matters: three numbers hedge a curve, and a spread is a bet

A curve has eight to thirty quoted points, and hedging each one separately is neither possible nor necessary — because they do not move independently. Three principal components explain around 98% of the daily variation in every developed-market curve ever studied, and they have the same interpretation every time: level, slope, curvature. That is the single most useful empirical fact in fixed income, and it is a two-line sklearn call on the panel you built in the lab.

What the hedger buys with this. If three factors explain 98% of curve variance, a book with zero PC1, PC2 and PC3 exposure has almost no curve risk — so you hedge three numbers instead of thirty, using three liquid instruments. The residual 2% is basis risk, and you size it rather than ignore it. Week 4's discipline applies unchanged: report the residual, do not assume it away.

Interactive · a credit spread as an implied default probability

The credit triangle: spread ≈ PD × (1−recovery). Invert it and a quoted spread becomes an implied annual default probability — under an assumption about recovery that nobody can observe until it is too late. Compare the implied number to the realised historical default rate for that rating and the gap is the risk premium you are being paid, which for investment grade is most of the spread.

Part 4

4Five ways this goes wrong

All five have shipped in a submitted curve notebook. Open them.

Discounting with par yields instead of bootstrapped zeros

The ten-year par yield is the internal rate of return of a particular cash-flow schedule. Using it to discount a five-year coupon prices that coupon off the wrong point of the curve. On a steep curve the error on a long bond is 40–90 bp of price, which is an order of magnitude larger than any bid-ask you will ever cross.

The same error in relative-value form is worse: two bonds with the same maturity and different coupons have different yields on a sloped curve, and a "cheap/rich" screen built on yield differences is measuring the coupon, not value.

Fix: bootstrap once, store DF(t), and price everything as cf @ DF. Then quote yields as an output, never use them as an input.

DGS10 is not a zero rate, and FRED's NaNs are holidays

FRED's DGS* series are constant-maturity par yields interpolated by Treasury from the on-the-run curve — semi-annual, bond-equivalent, and not a zero. Treating them as zeros skips the bootstrap altogether and mis-states long discount factors by tens of basis points.

And every one of those series has . on market holidays, which pandas-datareader hands back as NaN. A careless ffill across a Friday–Monday gap creates a zero-change day; a careless dropna(how="any") across eight tenors silently deletes any day where a single tenor was not published.

Fix: state the convention in a comment, bootstrap properly, and align tenors with an explicit business-day calendar. Count and report how many days you dropped, and why.

Hedging a curve move with duration alone

Duration is the first derivative, so a duration-matched hedge is exact only for an infinitesimal parallel shift. Two things break it. Convexity: on a 200 bp move the second-order term is worth 1.5–4 points on a long bond, and it is always in your favour if you are long the convex leg and against you if you are short it.

And curve risk: a portfolio long the 2-year and short the 10-year can be perfectly duration-neutral and still lose money on every flattening, because PC2 exposure is not PC1 exposure. A "duration-hedged" barbell is a leveraged bet on curvature.

Fix: report key-rate durations or PC1/PC2/PC3 exposures, not one duration number, and include the convexity term whenever the scenario exceeds about 50 bp. The widget in Part 2 shows you exactly where the linear approximation crosses your tolerance.

Reading the breakeven as expected inflation, full stop

The breakeven equals expected inflation plus an inflation-risk premium minus a TIPS liquidity premium. In calm markets the two premia partly offset and the reading is a decent proxy. In a liquidity event they do not: in March 2020 the 5-year breakeven fell from 1.7% to under 0.2% in three weeks, and most of that was TIPS being dumped by funds that needed cash, not the market forecasting deflation.

Its cousin: the naive subtraction. n − r is a first-order approximation to (1+n)/(1+r) − 1; at a 4% nominal and 1.8% real the difference is only about 4 bp, but it is a free correction and graders notice.

Fix: use the exact Fisher ratio, and whenever you quote a breakeven, quote it as "breakeven, not expected inflation" and say which way the premia are likely to be pushing. Cross-check against FRED's own T5YIE.

Inverting the credit triangle and forgetting that recovery is an assumption

PD = s/(1−R) is exact under a flat-hazard, risk-neutral model, and every term on the right is a choice. Move recovery from 40% to 25% and the implied PD falls by a fifth; the market quote did not change at all. Reporting an implied PD without the recovery assumption next to it is reporting half a number.

Second, the implied PD is risk-neutral. For investment grade it runs 3–8× the realised historical default rate, because the spread also pays for liquidity, ratings-migration risk and the fact that defaults cluster in bad states of the world. Comparing an implied PD to a historical table and concluding "the market is wrong" is the classic first-year error.

Fix: always publish the triple — spread, recovery assumption, implied PD — and show the implied/realised multiple alongside. Then say which part you think is default compensation and which is premium, and size accordingly.
