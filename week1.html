WEEK 1

Financial Data & Returns

Every number you will compute for the next nine weeks is a function of one object: a panel of returns. This week you build it, and then you break it four ways — because a factor loading, a VaR and a Sharpe ratio are all perfectly happy to be computed from a panel that is quietly wrong.

  • Re-derive a stock's split and dividend adjustment from the corporate-action history and reconcile it against Adj Close to a stated tolerance.
  • Choose log or simple returns for a reason, and know which one you are allowed to add across time and which across assets.
  • Build a return panel from a daily S&P-100 download, align it on a real trading calendar, and then produce a fake Sharpe of 4 with a single mis-specified join.
  • Quantify what survivorship is worth in basis points a year on your own universe, instead of citing somebody else's number.

Part 1

1What is it? A price is not a return, and a close is not a total return

Three different series get called "the price of the stock", and they disagree by tens of percent over a few years. The raw close is what actually printed on the tape. The adjusted close is a back-rewritten history whose returns match what a holder experienced. The total-return index is that holder's wealth, dividends reinvested. Only the last two produce a return you can put in a panel — and the middle one is recomputed every time a new action occurs, which is why a cached signal can silently become unreproducible.

Raw close

What printed. Drops by roughly the dividend on the ex-date and by the split factor on the split date. Use it for anything about tradability — tick size, round lots, the actual quote — and for nothing about performance.

Adjusted close

Every historical price multiplied by the cumulative factor of all later actions, so that adj.pct_change() equals the holder's return. Convenient, and a moving target: it changes whenever a new dividend is declared.

Total-return index

Start with one share, reinvest every dividend at the ex-date price, multiply through every split. The only series that is a wealth path. This is what a benchmark provider actually publishes.

Interactive · log or simple? Add across what?

Both are correct; each is additive along exactly one axis. Log returns add across time and simple returns average across assets — and using the wrong one is a silent error that grows with the size of the moves. Pick a sample and read the gap in basis points.

The sample

Scaling up is the point: at daily-sized moves log and simple agree to within a basis point, and the whole distinction looks pedantic. At monthly or crisis-sized moves it is worth percent.

Three assets, one period

Weight on C is whatever is left over. The portfolio's simple return is the weighted average of the simple returns — always. The weighted average of the log returns is not the portfolio's log return, and the gap is a Jensen term.

Part 2

  
2How you build it — and the two joins that invent a profit

  
A return panel is a DataFrame indexed by trading day with one column per
  name. Building it is four lines of pandas. Building it correctly is a set of
  decisions about calendars, missing data, delistings and the exact moment each column becomes knowable —
  and two of those decisions can manufacture a Sharpe ratio out of nothing.


  

    

      
The four lines

      
px = yf.download(TICKERS, start="2019-01-01",
                 end="2024-01-01",
                 auto_adjust=False)
adj = px["Adj Close"].sort_index()
ret = np.log(adj).diff()          # log returns
ret = ret.dropna(how="all")      # drop dead rows

      
Note auto_adjust=False. The modern default
      adjusts Close in place and throws the raw close away — which makes the reconciliation
      exercise impossible, because you no longer have the series you are trying to reconcile.

    

    

      
The five decisions hidden in them

      
    
        
  • Calendar. Whose trading days? A union index quietly forward-fills foreign holidays into
        zero-return days that halve your measured vol.
    • 
        
  • Missing data. NaN means "not knowable", not "0.0". Filling with zero is a
        return; filling forward is a price.
    • 
        
  • Universe as of when. Today's index members, or the members on each date?
    • 
        
  • Knowability. The instant each cell becomes available to a decision, which is never the
        timestamp on the row.
    • 
        
  • Adjustment vintage. Which day's Adj Close — because tomorrow's differs.
    •

Part 3

  
3Why it matters: the decision the panel actually makes

  
A panel is not an artefact you hand in; it is the thing an allocation, a hedge ratio and
  a risk limit are computed from. Here is what each of this week's four errors does downstream — and note
  that none of them shows up as an exception, a warning, or a number that looks
  strange.


  

    

      
The allocation decision

      
Suppose your survivorship-inflated panel says small-cap value has returned 11.4% a
      year with 18% vol, against 8.9%/18% for the true universe. That 250 bps is not a rounding
      error: at a 60/40 starting point it is the difference between recommending a 15% sleeve and a 0%
      sleeve, because the optimiser in week 3 will size the position on exactly that mean.

      
Estimation error in the mean is already the weakest link in mean-variance. Adding a
      systematic upward bias to it is the single most expensive data error in this
      course.

    

    

      
The risk decision

      
Forward-filling a foreign holiday inserts a zero return. Twelve of those in a year
      cut measured variance by about 5%, so an annualised vol of 20% is reported as 19.5% — and a VaR
      scaled from it is understated on exactly the days the market was shut for a reason.

      
Worse, zeros are inserted non-randomly: they cluster on holidays
      and on halted names, which is when the tail actually lives. Week 4's exceedance backtest is the only
      thing that catches this, and it catches it late.

    

    

      
The factor decision

      
Regress an unadjusted price series on the market and a 4-for-1 split becomes a −75%
      day. One such observation in 756 is enough to drag a beta of 1.05 down to 0.7 and to hand you a
      hugely "significant" negative alpha. Week 2's diagnostics — leverage, Cook's distance, a residual
      plot you actually look at — exist for this.

    

    

      
The execution decision

      
The look-ahead join is the error that survives longest, because it makes everything
      downstream look better: the model fits, the backtest is profitable, the risk
      report is calm. It dies in week 9, on the tape, where the fill prices are timestamped and the
      signal's arrival time is a matter of record.

Part 4

  
4Five ways this goes wrong

  
Every one of these has shipped in a submitted notebook. Open them.


  
auto_adjust=True, and then "reconciling" the adjustment
    

      
Recent yfinance defaults to adjusting in place: Close comes back
      already adjusted and there is no separate Adj Close column at all. Students then
      "reconcile" the adjusted series against itself, get a maximum error of exactly 0.0, and report a
      perfect result.

      
The tell is a reconciliation that is too good. A real one has a handful of
      rows off by 1e-6 from rounding, and usually one genuine mismatch on a special dividend that Yahoo
      classifies differently from you.

      
Fix: auto_adjust=False, keep both columns, and assert that
      (px["Close"] != px["Adj Close"]).any() before you claim to have reconciled anything.

    


  
Summing simple returns, or averaging log returns across a portfolio
    

      
Two mirror-image errors. ret.sum() on simple returns is not the holding-period
      return — over a year of 10%-a-month moves it overstates by several percent. And
      (w * log_ret).sum() is not the portfolio's log return, because a portfolio is a linear
      combination of prices, not of logs.

      
Both errors are small enough at daily frequency to pass every eyeball test and large enough at
      monthly frequency to change a conclusion.

      
Fix: one rule, written at the top of the file. Aggregate across time in
      logs (log_ret.sum(), then expm1). Aggregate across assets in
      simple ((w * simple_ret).sum(), then convert to log if you need to compound it).

    


  
A ffill that turns a halt into a zero return
    

      
panel.ffill() on prices is defensible; the same call after differencing, or a
      fillna(0) on returns, is not. A zero return is a strong claim — that the asset traded
      and did not move — and it is almost always false where the NaN was.

      
Because the gaps cluster on holidays, halts and delistings, the inserted zeros shrink your
      variance estimate exactly where the tail lives. Measured vol falls by roughly
      n_zeros / n_obs in variance terms; a VaR scaled from that vol inherits the whole
      error.

      
Fix: reindex onto one explicit exchange calendar, leave genuine gaps as
      NaN, and make every downstream estimator take min_periods seriously.
      Report the missing-data rate per column next to every vol you publish.

    


  
Today's index members, yesterday's history
    

      
tickers = current_sp100() then five years of history is survivorship bias in one
      line, and it is worth 100–300 bps a year depending on the universe and the decade. Enron,
      Lehman and Wirecard are simply not in your download.

      
Its sibling is subtler: a name that joined the index in 2022 because it went
      up 400% brings that 400% with it into your 2019 panel. That is selection on the dependent
      variable.

      
Fix: point-in-time membership, or say plainly in the tearsheet that the universe is
      as-of-today and quantify the bias on your own data with the simulator above. An honest, measured
      bias is acceptable; an unmentioned one is not.

    


  
A signal joined on the day it describes, not the day it arrives
    

      
The most profitable bug in finance. Fundamentals dated to the quarter end but filed six weeks
      later; a vendor file stamped with the observation date; an intraday feature computed from the day's
      close and joined to that same day's return. Every one of them yields a Sharpe between 3 and 8 and a
      backtest that no live account will ever reproduce.

      
It hides because it makes nothing fail. It also hides in the other direction: a genuine one-day
      edge can be destroyed by a lazy shift(1) too many, which looks like the strategy simply
      not working.

      
Fix: every feature carries an explicit as-of timestamp separate
      from the date it describes, the join is on the as-of, and execution happens on the bar after the
      decision. Then prove it: shuffle the signal's dates and confirm the P&L goes to zero.
