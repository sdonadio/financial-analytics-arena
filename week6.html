WEEK 6

Derivatives Analytics

An option price is one number that contains a whole distribution. This week you build the machine that turns the two into each other — Black-Scholes forward for a price, bisection backward for an implied vol — and then you look at what the market's own prices say about the model: a smile that should be flat, and an arbitrage that is only a stale quote.

  • Draw the payoff of any combination of legs and separate it from the value of that combination before expiry.
  • Implement Black-Scholes and its Greeks, and verify your code against a number you can check: S=100, K=100, r=5%, σ=20%, T=1 gives a call of 10.4506.
  • Invert the formula by bisection to get implied vol from a price, and know why you bisect on vega-positive functions rather than trusting Newton.
  • Build the smile and the surface from a real option chain, and read the skew as a statement about the left tail rather than a bug.
  • Test put-call parity, find an arbitrage, and then explain why it is not one.

Part 1

1What is it? A payoff, and then a price for it

Everything in derivatives analytics separates into two questions. The first is arithmetic: what does this thing pay at expiry, as a function of the underlying? That is a piecewise-linear graph you can draw with no model at all. The second is the hard one: what is it worth now, before the uncertainty resolves — and that requires a model of how the underlying moves.

Payoff

Model-free. A call pays max(Sₜ−K, 0) at expiry and nothing before it. Add legs and the payoff adds: every structure in the widget below is a sum of at most four of these kinks.

Price

Model-dependent. The value today is the discounted expected payoff under a risk-neutral measure, and Black-Scholes is one particular choice of measure — lognormal, constant vol, continuous trading, no jumps.

Intrinsic and time value

Price minus intrinsic is time value, and it is what you are actually trading. It peaks at the money, decays to zero at expiry, and is where every Greek lives.

Part 2

2How you build it — forward for a price, backward for a vol

Black-Scholes is eight lines of code and one thing to get right: the two d terms. Write it, then check it against a number. At S=100, K=100, r=5%, σ=20%, T=1 the European call is worth 10.4506 and the put is worth 5.5735. If your code does not reproduce those to four decimals, stop and fix it before you invert anything.

The formula, and the Greeks that come free

from scipy.stats import norm

def bs_call(S, K, r, sig, T):
    sT = sig * np.sqrt(T)
    d1 = (np.log(S / K) + (r + sig**2 / 2) * T) / sT
    d2 = d1 - sT
    return S * norm.cdf(d1) - K * np.exp(-r * T) * norm.cdf(d2)

# the same d1, d2 give every Greek
delta = norm.cdf(d1)
gamma = norm.pdf(d1) / (S * sT)
vega  = S * norm.pdf(d1) * np.sqrt(T)      # per 1.00 vol
theta = (-S * norm.pdf(d1) * sig / (2 * np.sqrt(T))
         - r * K * np.exp(-r * T) * norm.cdf(d2))

assert abs(bs_call(100, 100, 0.05, 0.20, 1) - 10.4506) < 1e-4

That last line is the whole quality-control regime for this week. Put it in a test file; every later widget on this page is checked against it.

Inverting it: bisection, not Newton

def iv(price, S, K, r, T, lo=1e-4, hi=5.0):
    for _ in range(100):
        mid = (lo + hi) / 2
        if bs_call(S, K, r, mid, T) > price:
            hi = mid
        else:
            lo = mid
    return (lo + hi) / 2

The call price is strictly increasing in σ — vega is positive everywhere — so bisection cannot fail to converge, and it halves the bracket every step: 40 iterations gets you to 5×10−13. Newton is faster and diverges on deep out-of-the-money quotes where vega is near zero, which is exactly where a real chain is full of stale prices.

Before you invert, check the no-arbitrage bounds: max(S−Ke−rT, 0) ≤ C ≤ S. A quote outside them has no implied vol at all, and a mid computed from a one-sided book will breach them often.

Part 3

3Why it matters: the smile is the model failing in public

If Black-Scholes were true, every option on the same underlying with the same expiry would imply the same volatility. They do not. Equity index options have implied vols that fall as the strike rises — the skew — and rise again far out of the money, and the whole shape changes with maturity. That surface is not an error in your inversion. It is the market pricing a left tail, a jump risk and a demand for crash protection that a lognormal model has no way to express.

So why keep using the model?

Because implied vol is a quoting convention. Nobody believes the lognormal assumption; everybody uses Black-Scholes as an invertible map between a dollar price and a number that is comparable across strikes, maturities and underlyings. A trader who says "I bought 22 vol" is quoting a price through a model they know is wrong, on purpose.

What the shape tells you

The level is the price of variance. The skew is the price of the left tail — steeper skew means crash protection is getting expensive. The convexity (the smile's curl) prices the probability of a large move in either direction. Those three numbers are the equity-vol analogue of week 5's level, slope and curvature.

Interactive · put-call parity, and the arbitrage that is not one

Parity is model-free: C − P = S − Ke−rT, by a static replication argument that holds whatever the underlying does. So a violation is either free money or bad data — and in a retail option chain it is always bad data. Turn on stale quotes to see the classic version: the underlying has moved, the option quotes have not, and parity appears to break by 60 cents.

TermValueNote
—

The rule to write in the notebook. Before you report a parity violation, check three things in order: is the underlying quote from the same instant as the option quotes; is the option price a mid of a two-sided book with real size; and does the apparent edge survive two bid-ask spreads plus fees plus the borrow on the short leg. In a free retail chain, one of those three kills it every time.
Part 4

4Five ways this goes wrong

Every one of these has appeared in a submitted option-chain notebook. Open them.

Inverting the mid of a one-sided book — and getting a 300% implied vol

A free option chain contains rows with a bid of 0.00 and an ask of 0.05, rows that last traded three weeks ago, and rows where the "price" is the last trade rather than a mid. Invert those and you get implied vols of 4%, 300%, or nothing at all — and then a single garbage point drags your smile fit into a shape no market ever quoted.

The tell is a surface with spikes at the far wings and at the shortest expiry, which is exactly where liquidity dies.

Fix: filter before you invert. Require a two-sided quote, a positive bid, a relative spread under some threshold, non-zero open interest, and a price inside the no-arbitrage bounds max(S−Ke−rT,0) ≤ C ≤ S. Report how many rows you dropped and why — that count is part of the result.

Forgetting that American equity options are not European

Every single-name US equity option is American. Black-Scholes prices a European claim. For calls on a non-dividend payer the difference is zero (early exercise is never optimal), which is why the error hides — but for puts, and for calls across an ex-dividend date, the American option is strictly worth more, and inverting a European formula on it produces an implied vol biased upward.

The bias is small for short, near-the-money options and material for deep in-the-money puts, which is where a naive script finds its most exciting "skew".

Fix: state the assumption. Restrict the fit to out-of-the-money options — where early exercise is worth almost nothing and the quotes are liquid — or price with a binomial tree if you need the in-the-money wing. Handle the dividend explicitly with q.

Newton-Raphson on the wing, and a divide-by-near-zero

Newton's update is σ ← σ − (C(σ)−P)/vega. Deep out of the money, vega is 1e-8, so one iteration throws σ to 40 or to a negative number, and the next call to log or sqrt returns NaN. The NaN then propagates silently through your surface fit.

The same instability is why a vectorised Newton over a whole chain quietly returns a few NaNs that a later .mean() turns into one NaN for the entire tenor.

Fix: bisect. It is monotone, it cannot diverge, and 60 iterations is still microseconds. If you want speed, bracket with bisection first and polish with two Newton steps — and assert on the final price error rather than trusting convergence.

Reading the skew as a forecast

A steep put skew says out-of-the-money puts are expensive relative to a lognormal benchmark. It does not say the market expects a crash. Most of the skew is a risk premium: the people who sell crash protection demand to be paid for it, and index puts have earned a persistently negative return for decades precisely because that premium is real.

The mirror error: computing a risk-neutral density from the surface and calling it "the market's probability distribution". It is the risk-neutral distribution, which differs from the real-world one by exactly the risk premium you are trying to measure.

Fix: keep the two measures separate in your writing. Say "risk-neutral" every time you mean it, and if you want to make a claim about real-world probabilities, you need an assumption about the pricing kernel — so state it.

Testing parity with prices from three different timestamps

The underlying quote comes from one API call, the call chain from a second, the put chain from a third, and the three arrive up to a few seconds apart. If the underlying has moved 30 cents in between, parity appears violated by roughly the delta-weighted amount of that move — around 30 cents at the money — and it will look like a persistent, tradable edge because the bias has a sign in a trending market.

The second-order version: using an annual rate with the wrong compounding, or a T measured in calendar days when the quotes convention is business days. Both shift the right-hand side by a few cents, which is enough to manufacture an "edge" of the same size as the one you are hunting.

Fix: one snapshot, one timestamp, recorded. Compare the parity residual to two full spreads plus fees before you use the word arbitrage, and plot the residual against moneyness — a real data problem is a smooth function of delta, and free money is not.
