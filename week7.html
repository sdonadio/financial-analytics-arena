WEEK 7

Backtesting & Performance Attribution

A backtest is not an experiment. It is a claim — that if you had been in the market, with this signal, paying these costs, this is what would have happened. This week you take one signal and watch a single .shift(1) move its Sharpe from +3.10 to −3.48, then subtract the spread, then the fee, then count how many configurations you tried, and finally split what survives into allocation, selection and interaction with a residual of exactly zero.

  • Build the same backtest twice — vectorised and event-driven — and find the look-ahead bug that lives on exactly one line of the vectorised version.
  • Decompose a loss into its parts: on the arena tape, −8,600 is spread −2,127, fee −6,473 and lag −1.35 — and defend the claim that the lag term is small because the alpha is zero, not because your execution is fast.
  • Compute Sharpe, Sortino and the deflated Sharpe that knows how many things you tried, and say why the run that lost 103% of the account has the best Sharpe of four.
  • Run a genuine walk-forward and watch a best-in-sample +2.87 become a mean out-of-sample +0.45.
  • Reconcile a Brinson-Fachler attribution to the active return, name your convention, and treat a non-zero residual as the bug it is.

Part 1

1What is it? Two architectures, and only one of them can lie quietly

A backtest replays a decision rule over historical data and reports what it would have earned. There are exactly two ways to build one, they answer the same question, and they fail in completely different ways — which is why this course makes you write both.

Vectorised

Weights and returns are two aligned matrices; P&L is one elementwise product and a row sum. Three lines, a hundred thousand bars, milliseconds.

  • Fast enough to sweep a parameter grid over lunch.
  • Every position is filled, in full, at the price on the bar — no queue, no partial, no book.
  • Alignment is the whole risk. The difference between an honest backtest and a time machine is whether one series was shifted by one row.

Event-driven

A loop over messages. A snapshot arrives, the strategy is asked for an order, the order goes into a queue, and it fills against the book on a later message.

  • The causal order is structural — you cannot accidentally trade on a bar you have not seen yet, because the bar has not arrived.
  • It models what vectorised code assumes away: partial fills, displayed size, queue position, rejects.
  • Slower, more code, and it hides its own bugs in the controller rather than in the alignment — as you will see when a missing in-flight-order check turns 868 fills into 8,405.
The course’s rule. Prototype vectorised, confirm event-driven, and reconcile the two before you believe either. On the AlgoArena tape the reconciliation target is the change in net worth — cash plus mark-to-mid — because that number is sign-correct even when the harness’s own realised-P&L line is not.

The four questions a backtest exists to answer

  • How much did it earn? Gross return, before anything is subtracted.
  • How much of that was me paying the market? Spread, fee, impact — each named and sourced, never one lump “cost assumption”.
  • Would I have believed it if I had been honest about my search? The trial count, and the deflated Sharpe at that count.
  • Where did it come from? Attribution — allocation, selection, interaction.

What the arena tape is

  • A session recording: every broadcast the exchange sent, in order — book_snapshot, trade_execution, session_event.
  • 4,000 ticks × 5 symbols = 20,000 book snapshots, about 5 MB.
  • It is broadcast-only: no order acknowledgements, no cancels, no per-team ladder. Nothing order-level can be reconstructed from it — remember that in week 9.
  • The offline recorder makes one without a network, so no result in this course depends on having attended a session.

Part 2

  
2How it works: the lag flip, then the cost ladder

  
Two mechanisms account for almost every backtest that cannot be reproduced live. The
  first is a leak that turns negative autocorrelation into fake alpha. The second is a cost floor that is
  usually larger than the edge it is subtracted from.


  
Widget 2 · the one-bar-lag flip

  
A mid computed from a two-sided book bounces: it sits nearer the bid, then nearer
  the ask, as makers requote. That bounce is mechanically negative autocorrelation — pooled
  AC(1) = −0.222 on the deck’s five-second bars, and the more negative the wider the
  market, with a rank correlation of −0.321 against half-spread. It is not a forecastable return, and
  it evaporates the instant you have to transact at the touch. Set the bounce and watch the sign of the
  Sharpe change with one row of alignment.

Lengthen the lookback and the flip weakens into a mere change of
  magnitude: bar t enters a 12-bar mean with one twelfth of the weight, so the leak is
  diluted even though it is still there. That is worth knowing and is not a defence —
  a diluted leak is still a leak, and you cannot tell from the output which part of the Sharpe it
  is.

  
Note which way the trap points. A leaky momentum
  backtest on a bouncing mid looks brilliant and is worthless. A leaky reversal backtest
  looks catastrophic and hides a real (if unharvestable) effect. The sign of your leak depends on the sign
  of your signal, so “my result looks bad, therefore I have not leaked” is not an argument.

Part 3

  
3Why it matters: how many things did you try?

  
Every statistic in a backtest is conditional on a search you did not report. Walk-forward
  measures the damage; the deflated Sharpe prices it; attribution says where whatever survives came
  from.


  
Widget 4 · in-sample versus walk-forward

Widget 5 · the deflated Sharpe ratio

  
Bailey and López de Prado’s correction asks the only question that matters about a backtest:
  how many did you try? If you try one coin-flip strategy its expected Sharpe is zero;
  if you try a hundred and keep the best, the best has a large positive Sharpe by construction. So the
  correct null is not “Sharpe = 0”, it is “Sharpe = SR₀(M)”, the expected
  maximum of M trials with no edge at all — which grows like the square
  root of log M. Then the third and fourth moments of the winner’s returns enter the denominator,
  because a Sharpe built from a handful of enormous bars is worth less than one built from many small
  ones.

Widget 6 · Brinson-Fachler, and the residual that must be zero

  
Sharpe judges a curve; attribution explains it. Split the active return into
  allocation — you were in the right sectors —
  selection — you owned the right names inside them — and interaction,
  the cross term that is usually negative because people overweight exactly where their timing is worst.
  The three sum to the active return as an algebraic identity, so any residual is a
  bug: almost always a weight and a return averaged over different windows, or a zero-weight name whose
  division produced a NaN that pandas quietly dropped.

Switch the convention and the total does not move by a basis
      point while allocation and selection both do. BHB measures allocation against the sector’s own
      return; Brinson-Fachler measures it against the benchmark total, which makes
      “overweight a sector that beat the benchmark” the thing you get paid for. A report that does
      not name its convention is not reproducible.

      
Single period only. Chaining periods needs a linking scheme — Carino,
      Menchero — because the components compound and the naive sum drifts from the geometric excess. Do not
      chain by adding.

    

  


  

    
Sharpe cannot see ruin

      
Of the four curves in the lab, the one that lost more than the entire
      account — −103.47%, max drawdown 103.51% — has the best Sharpe of the
      four at −0.005, and a PSR of 0.295. As net worth approached zero its per-mark returns became
      large and symmetric, and Sharpe is a ratio of moments. Report max drawdown next to every Sharpe you
      ever quote, and never annualise a per-mark ratio across a tape that spans a few seconds of wall
      clock: multiply by √252 and you have invented a year.

    
Sortino is a skewness detector

      
Sortino is Sharpe with downside deviation in the denominator, so it flatters anything
      with positive skew. Variant D’s Sortino is 2.9× its Sharpe — that ratio is not a
      second opinion about quality, it is a measurement of asymmetry. Quote both and the reader learns
      something; quote only the larger and you are selling.

  







  
Part 4

  
4Five ways this goes wrong

  
Every one of these has appeared in a submitted backtest. Open them.


  
Trading on the bar you used to decide — the whole bug is one missing .shift(1)
    

      
Compute a signal from the returns up to and including bar t, then multiply it by the
      return of bar t. Nothing errors, nothing looks odd, and on a bouncing mid the result is a
      Sharpe of +3.10 that reverses to −3.48 the moment the weights are shifted by one row. The leak
      is worth 169 percentage points of total return on 373 bars.

      
Its subtler siblings: a feature built with a centred rolling window; a forward-filled price that
      carries tomorrow’s close backwards over a gap; a cross-sectional rank computed over the full sample
      instead of per date.

      
Fix: build the event-driven version and reconcile. If a vectorised backtest and an
      event-driven one agree to a basis point, your alignment is right; if they disagree, the vectorised one
      is optimistic and you now know by how much. Assert it in a test with a series whose future you
      control.

    


  
One “cost assumption” instead of three measured terms
    

      
“We assumed 10 bps of costs” is a number somebody made up. The tape says the mean half-spread is
      12.2 bps, the venue’s config says the taker fee is 15 bps, and the per-symbol vector matters because
      META at 2.7 bps and NFLX at 20.8 bps are not the same instrument. Lumping them hides the ranking, and
      the ranking is the finding: on this venue the fee is three times the spread.

      
The mirror error is charging costs on notional traded when your strategy is a
      maker. Quoting at the touch turns the half-spread from a cost into a rebate and flips
      the sign of the whole identity — but only if your fill model can honestly claim you were filled, which
      a backtest that ignores queue position cannot.

      
Fix: publish the whole ladder — gross, minus spread, minus fee, minus impact — because
      the shape of the decay tells the reader whether the edge is real or a cost
      artefact. Name and source every term, and state whether you were maker or taker.

    


  
Reporting the best of a grid search as though it were a single result
    

      
Twenty-four configurations is a modest sweep for a student project. Regressing P&L on fill count
      across those 24 runs gave R² = 0.9966 and a slope of −$2.91 a fill, which
      on 20-share orders is 14.5 cents a share against a 15-cent half-spread. The “parameter search”
      explored a space of trading volumes, and its winner was the configuration that never trades.

      
Then the deflated Sharpe: with only six trials, luck alone produces a Sharpe of +1.12, so +2.87
      clears the bar. Six is a suspiciously honest count. Ask what SR₀ looks like at
      M = 200 — the number you will actually have run by the time Phase 2 is due,
      counting the ones you deleted.

      
Fix: log every configuration you run, including abandoned ones, and report the count as
      the --trials argument. Then show the P&L-versus-turnover regression: if R² is
      high, you measured your cost model, not your idea.

    


  
An attribution whose parts do not add to the change in net worth
    

      
The lab’s harness reports P&L −2,126.95 and
      Realized −4,294.11 with the book flat and unrealised zero. Flat plus zero
      unrealised means realised P&L is the P&L, so the two lines disagree by
      $2,167 and at most one is right: apply_buy computes
      old_qty = max(0, position), so buying back a short realises nothing and resets the
      average cost as if opening a fresh long. The realised line is long-only; net worth is
      sign-correct.

      
The same failure in attribution: allocation + selection + interaction that misses the active return
      by a basis point. That is not rounding, it is a weight and a return measured over different subsets,
      or a NaN dropped by a .sum().

      
Fix: trust the cash. Reconcile every decomposition to the change in
      net worth and assert the residual is zero in your test suite. Chasing the last basis point is where
      the real errors live.

    


  
Concluding “our execution is fast” from a lag sweep that measures nothing
    

      
Sweeping the execution lag over 0, 1, 5, 25 and 100 ticks moved P&L from −8,600.27 to
      −8,634.99. A hundred ticks of latency cost 0.4% of the loss. The flattering reading is that the
      execution is excellent; the correct reading is that lag destroys P&L in proportion to how fast
      your alpha decays, and this signal has none. A lag term that measures zero is evidence your
      signal is flat, not that your pipes are fast.

      
The opposite failure hides in the same experiment. Adding a naive one-bar lag turned a 2% loser
      into a −103.5% loser with a negative account balance and 18,670 signals against 868 — because
      the controller compared its target against a position that had not moved yet and re-issued the same
      order every tick. No traceback, no exception: the classic production bug is
      no in-flight order accounting, and it cost $79,684 in fees.

      
Fix: treat a queued order as already filled when computing the next target, and put
      signal count next to fill count in every report. A signal-to-fill ratio above about 2 is a controller
      bug until proven otherwise.
