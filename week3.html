WEEK 3

Portfolio Analytics & Optimisation

Markowitz gives you the exact answer to a question nobody can ask, because it needs the true means and the true covariance and you have neither. This week is about what to do instead — shrink the estimate, throw away the means, or refuse to optimise at all — and about why 1/N is not a joke benchmark.

  • Build a mean-variance frontier from a real return panel, and then resample it and watch it move by more than the effect you were trying to exploit.
  • Apply Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage to a covariance matrix and say, in one sentence, what the shrinkage intensity is buying you.
  • Race four portfolios — MVO, shrunk MVO, risk parity, 1/N — out of sample, with turnover counted and costs charged.
  • Impose the constraints a real mandate has (long-only, position caps) and report what they cost, in Sharpe and in effective breadth.

Part 1

1What is it? Two inputs, one quadratic program, and a lot of estimation error

Mean-variance optimisation is a one-line problem: minimise w'Σw subject to w'μ = m and 1'w = 1. It is also, in practice, an error-maximising machine — because the optimiser cannot tell a genuinely high expected return from an overestimated one, and it will happily put 40% of your book in whichever asset got lucky in your sample.

What you need

A vector of expected returns μ (n numbers) and a covariance matrix Σ (n(n+1)/2 numbers). For 30 names that is 495 parameters estimated from, if you are lucky, 120 monthly observations.

Which is harder

The means, by a wide margin. Estimating an annual mean to ±1% needs decades; estimating a covariance to a usable tolerance needs a few years. That asymmetry is why min-variance and risk parity — which never touch μ — are so competitive.

What goes wrong

Σ from n names and T < n periods is singular: the optimiser finds a portfolio with apparently zero variance and levers into it. Even with T > n, the smallest eigenvalues are the noisiest, and Σ⁻¹ multiplies exactly those.

Part 3

  
3Why it matters: the weight is the decision

  
Every earlier week produces an estimate; this is the week the estimate becomes an
  action. A number in a table is free to be wrong. A weight is not: it is capital, it
  has a cost to acquire, and it is the thing your risk report in week 4 is about to stress.


  

    

      
Why 1/N is the benchmark, not the joke

      
1/N has no parameters, so it has no estimation error, so it cannot be wrong in the
      way MVO is wrong. DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal’s result — that across many datasets 1/N beats
      sample-based mean-variance out of sample — is not a claim that optimisation is useless. It is a
      claim that the error in the inputs dominates the gain from optimising unless you
      do something about the error.

      
So the honest ranking of effort is: fix the inputs (shrinkage, factor models,
      constraints), then optimise. Optimising harder on bad inputs makes things worse, precisely and
      measurably.

    

    

      
What a constraint really is

      
A 10% position cap looks like a compliance nuisance. Statistically it is a
      prior: it says “I do not believe any single estimate enough to bet more than 10%
      on it”. Jagannathan and Ma showed that a long-only constraint on min-variance is equivalent to
      shrinking the covariance matrix — the constraint is the regularisation.

      
Which is why the constraint widget above reports how often the mandate
      raises the true Sharpe across 200 fresh samples, rather than asserting that it
      does: the answer depends on how noisy your inputs are relative to the real dispersion in expected
      returns. Shorten the estimation window and the constraint wins more often. If binding your
      optimiser makes the out-of-sample result better, your inputs were the problem — not the
      optimiser.

    

    

      
The number your tearsheet has to carry

      
Not the optimal Sharpe. The out-of-sample Sharpe net of costs, against 1/N, with
      turnover stated. Phase 1 asks for exactly that comparison, and a portfolio that loses to 1/N
      after costs is a finding — a publishable, defensible one — provided you say so.

    

    

      
What week 4 does with it

      
Takes these weights and asks how badly they can behave: the return distribution’s
      left tail, VaR and Expected Shortfall, the drawdown, and whether the covariance matrix you shrank
      here is still the right one in a crisis. Correlations go to one exactly when your diversification
      was the argument for the weights.

Part 4

  
4Five ways this goes wrong

  
Every one of these produces a beautiful in-sample number. Open them.


  
Optimising on the same sample you evaluate on
    

      
Fit μ and Σ on 2015–2024, optimise, then report the Sharpe of that portfolio over 2015–2024.
      The result is not a backtest, it is the definition of the tangency portfolio: it is
      guaranteed to have the highest Sharpe in that sample, by construction, whatever
      the data was.

      
This is the single most common error in submitted bake-offs, and it is invisible because the
      number is plausible — a Sharpe of 1.3 rather than 6.

      
Fix: weights from a trailing window, returns from the period after it, always. If a
      method has no out-of-sample column, it has no column.

    


  
A singular covariance matrix, and an optimiser that does not complain
    

      
With 50 names and 36 monthly observations, the sample covariance has rank 36 and is singular.
      np.linalg.inv will still return something — an enormous, arbitrary something — and the
      optimiser will find a “zero-variance” portfolio built entirely out of the null space.

      
The tell is weights of ±800%, a reported portfolio vol of 0.4%, and an in-sample Sharpe in the
      double digits.

      
Fix: shrink, or use a factor covariance, or use fewer names. And log
      np.linalg.cond(Sigma) at every rebalance — above about 1e4 you are inverting
      noise. Prefer solve to inv, and never pinv silently.

    


  
Turnover that nobody counted
    

      
Unconstrained MVO on a 36-month window typically turns over 200–400% a year, because the
      estimated means move and the optimiser chases them. At 20 bps per unit traded that is
      40–80 bps a year of pure cost, which is most of whatever edge the optimisation found.

      
Risk parity turns over a fraction of that, and 1/N turns over almost nothing — which is a real,
      economic advantage, not an accounting technicality.

      
Fix: report turnover as a first-class metric next to Sharpe, charge it, and consider
      a no-trade band: rebalance only when a weight has drifted more than a threshold. Costs belong in
      the objective, not in a footnote.

    


  
Annualising by the wrong number, or mixing frequencies
    

      
Monthly covariance times 252. Daily means times 12. A vol of 5.2% that should be 18%. Every one
      of these has appeared in a submitted notebook, and each produces a self-consistent, entirely wrong
      set of weights — because the optimiser only cares about the ratio of μ to Σ,
      and mixing frequencies breaks exactly that ratio.

      
Fix: annualise once, at the boundary, in one function; assert that portfolio vol
      lands between 3% and 60% before you use the weights; and keep the frequency in the variable name
      (mu_ann, cov_m).

    


  
Treating the constraint as the enemy
    

      
“My optimiser wants 180% in one name and the cap won’t let it.” The optimiser does not want
      anything; it is reporting that your estimated mean for that name is enormous relative to its
      estimated risk, and that estimate is almost certainly noise.

      
Students often respond by loosening the cap until the “optimal” solution is allowed. The result
      reliably gets worse out of sample.

      
Fix: read a binding constraint as a diagnostic. If long-only or a 10% cap changes
      your portfolio materially, your inputs are too noisy to justify the unconstrained answer — so
      shrink harder, use a factor covariance, or drop the means. And report the constrained result as the
      headline, because it is the one you could actually run.
